Measure three times, and do not average

The single most useful habit in this entire project is measuring each wall at three heights — just above the pan, at about chest height, and near the top — and writing down all three numbers separately.

Almost everyone measures once, in the middle, and orders from that. It works in new construction where someone set the framing recently and checked it. It fails in remodels, because houses settle, framing twists, and the wall you are measuring may have been out of plumb since 1974.

The spread between those three numbers is the whole decision. Under an eighth of an inch and you can order exact-size panels. More than that and you want oversized panels you can scribe to the wall. Averaging the three numbers destroys exactly the information you needed, and produces a panel that fits perfectly at one height and leaves a visible gap at the other two.

Why the asymmetry matters so much: a panel cut too large is five minutes with a fine-tooth blade. A panel cut too small is scrap, a reorder, and two more weeks with the bathroom out of service. Every decision on this page tilts toward ordering slightly big for that reason alone.

Being on a stock footprint is worth more than it sounds. It means the widest choice of finishes, the shortest lead times, and the cheapest panels, because you are buying something the manufacturer already makes rather than something they have to set up for. Choosing a panel height

Heights run 72, 78, 80, 84, and 96 inches, with 108-inch panels available from a small number of manufacturers for nine-foot ceilings.

  • 72″ — the traditional height. Leaves roughly a foot of painted wall above the panel on an 8-foot ceiling, and that band is exactly where steam damage shows up first.
  • 78″ and 80″ — more coverage, still leaves a strip. A reasonable middle ground and a common stock height.
  • 84″ — matches a standard door height, which reads as deliberate rather than arbitrary. Still a painted band above.
  • 96″ — reaches the top of a standard 8-foot ceiling with no painted band at all. This is why it has become the default choice for anyone remodelling specifically to reduce maintenance.
  • 108″ — for 9-foot ceilings. Rare, expensive, and heavy enough that getting a sheet up a staircase is a genuine planning question. Cultured marble and solid surface are the realistic materials at this height.

The jump from 80 to 96 inches typically costs a few hundred dollars in material and removes a strip of wall that will otherwise need repainting every few years. It is one of the few upgrades in this category that reliably pays for itself, and we would take it over a material upgrade if the budget only stretched to one.

How square is square enough

Houses are not built to the tolerances that manufactured panels are made to, and after demolition the situation frequently gets worse rather than better.

The last row is worth taking seriously. Panels are flat and rigid; they do not disguise a leaning wall, they announce it, because a perfectly straight panel edge against a leaning ceiling line draws the eye straight to the problem. Furring out and re-plumbing a wall is not a large job compared with what you are already spending, and it is much cheaper before the panels arrive than after.

Check the corners too. Hold a framing square into each inside corner and look for daylight. A corner that is out of square by any meaningful amount affects how the side panel meets the back panel, and it is a different problem from a wall that leans.

Exact-size versus oversized

Most manufacturers offer both. Exact-size panels arrive finished on all edges and ready to install with no cutting. Oversized panels arrive deliberately large, with one or more unfinished edges intended to be trimmed on site.

Order exact-size when

You measured framing, not a finished wall

New construction, or a gut where you set the substrate yourself and checked it for plumb. Your three measurements agree within an eighth of an inch. You want factory-finished edges everywhere and no site cutting.

Order oversized when

Anything else, honestly

Remodel work, existing walls, measurements that vary at all, or any uncertainty about what you will find behind the current surface. This is what experienced installers order by default, and the small premium is cheap insurance.

The one genuine downside of oversized panels is that the trimmed edge is a cut edge rather than a factory edge, so it needs the trim profile or the silicone bead to cover it. In practice that edge is almost always in a corner or against the pan, where it is covered anyway.

Valve clearance, the thing people forget

Here is a problem that only exists with panels and only when going over an existing surface: every layer you add pushes the finished wall outward, and the valve does not move.

A valve stem projects a fixed distance from the framing. The original tile used some of that projection; a panel bonded on top of that tile uses more. If the total exceeds what the stem projects, the trim kit will not seat, and the fix is a valve extension kit or moving the valve forward, which means opening the wall.

Clearance check

Will the valve still work once the panel is on?

Going over existing tile adds thickness to the wall. That thickness has to come from somewhere, and the valve is usually where it runs out.

Measure stem projection from the current finished wall surface to the tip of the valve stem, with the trim removed. Escutcheon reach varies by manufacturer — treat this as a flag to check the spec sheet, not as the spec sheet.

Check this before you order the panel, not after. It can legitimately change which material you should buy — if clearance is tight, a 0.22-inch SPC panel and a 0.625-inch cultured marble panel are not interchangeable choices, regardless of which one you prefer.

The same arithmetic applies at three other places worth measuring: the shower door jamb, which may no longer sit flush; the pan edge, where the panel needs to lap the upstand; and any existing niche or shelf, whose opening gets smaller by the panel thickness on every side.

The measuring protocol

Twenty minutes with a tape, a level, and a framing square. Do it in this order.

  1. Back wall, three widths

    Just above the pan, at chest height, near the top. Corner to corner each time. Write down all three — do not average, do not round to the nearest half inch.

  2. Each side wall, three depths

    Same three heights. Side walls are frequently more out of square than the back wall, because they are shorter and often built against something else.

  3. Height, in two places per wall

    From the pan or tub deck up to where you want the panel to stop. Floors slope, pans slope, and ceilings are not always level relative to either.

  4. Check plumb and square

    Level on each wall vertically. Framing square into each inside corner. Note which direction anything leans, because that determines which edge gets scribed.

  5. Valve and showerhead positions

    Measure the centre of each from the pan edge and from the nearest corner. Use the pan as your reference, not an adjacent wall that may not be plumb. Note the valve stem projection with the trim removed.

  6. Test the substrate

    Press and tap across every wall. Soft, hollow, or moving means repair before surface. This is the step that changes budgets, so do it before you order anything.

  7. Photograph everything

    Corners, pan edge, valve, and any damage. When you send measurements for a quote, photos of the corners and the pan edge are far more useful than a wide shot of the room.

Tub surrounds measure differently

Two things change. First, measure the height from the tub deck, not the floor — tub heights vary by several inches between models and the deck is the reference the panels actually sit on. Second, the tub deck itself is rarely level, because tubs are set to drain.

Standard tub surround kits are built around a back wall up to 60 inches with returns to about 36 inches. Heights from the deck run 60, 66, 72, and 84 inches. As with showers, going taller removes the painted band that steam damages, and the material cost of doing so is modest.

The joint where the panel meets the tub deck is the most important one in the installation and the one most likely to have failed in whatever you are replacing, so inspect the substrate there closely before covering it. More on tub surrounds here.

Corner and neo-angle showers measure differently

An alcove is a rectangle with one open side, and it is forgiving because two of its three walls are held square by the walls of the room. A corner shower has only one corner formed by the building; the other edges are defined by glass, which means the panels have to be right rather than merely close.

Three differences worth planning for:

  • Only two walls get panelled. The return edges are exposed rather than tucked into a corner, so the edge finish becomes visible and matters far more than it does in an alcove. This is where an integral or bullnose edge earns its money over a plastic trim profile.
  • The glass has to land on something flat. A frameless panel or door sits against the panel face, so any bow or lippage in that panel becomes a sealing problem. Measure the flatness of those two walls with a straightedge, not just their dimensions.
  • Neo-angle geometry is not obvious from a tape measure. A neo-angle has a clipped corner at 135 degrees, and the angled face has to be dimensioned along its own length rather than derived from the two side dimensions. Measure the diagonal directly.

If you are working with a neo-angle, send us the pan model number along with the measurements. Pan manufacturers publish exact wall dimensions for their bases, and matching the panels to the published pan geometry is far more reliable than measuring a rough opening.

Ceilings, niches, and benches

Ceiling panels

A ceiling panel closes the top of the enclosure and removes the last painted surface in the wet zone. It is worth considering in two situations: a steam-heavy bathroom with marginal ventilation, where the ceiling above a shower is the first thing to grow mould, and any enclosure where you have gone to 96-inch walls and a painted ceiling would undercut the effect.

Measure the ceiling as its own rectangle, at all four edges, because a ceiling that looks square rarely is. Note that a ceiling panel needs mechanical support during curing, not just adhesive — gravity works against you in a way it does not on a wall, and this is one of the few parts of a panel installation that genuinely benefits from a professional.

Niches

Two options exist and they have very different measurement consequences. A pre-formed niche is a moulded unit that the panel cuts around, and its opening dimension is fixed by the manufacturer, so you are designing the wall around the niche. A site-built niche framed into the wall and then panel-lined needs each of its five faces measured individually, and each face loses the panel thickness on every side — which means the finished opening is meaningfully smaller than the framed opening.

That shrinkage catches people out. A niche framed at 12 by 24 inches, lined with half-inch solid surface on all faces, finishes at roughly 11 by 23. Frame generously.

Benches and seats

A bench changes the panel layout because the back panel now has to be cut around it, or stop above it, and that cut line has to be dead level or it will be the most visible line in the shower. Measure the bench height at both ends and at the front and back, since benches are usually sloped slightly for drainage.

If a fold-down seat is going in later rather than now, the relevant measurement is not the seat — it is the blocking behind the wall that the seat will anchor into, and that has to exist before the panel goes on. Same principle as grab bar blocking: decide it before the walls close, or take them apart later.

Six expensive mistakes

  1. Measuring once, in the middle. The most common error and the most expensive. It produces a panel that fits at one height and gaps at the other two.
  2. Averaging the three numbers. This throws away the exact information you took the trouble to gather. Record all three.
  3. Ordering exact-size for a remodel. Unless you set the substrate yourself and checked it, order oversized. The premium is small; the reorder is not.
  4. Measuring the valve from an adjacent wall. If that wall is out of plumb, so is your hole. Always reference the pan edge.
  5. Forgetting the panel thickness at the valve. Every layer added pushes the wall out. Run the clearance check before you order.
  6. Not testing the substrate first. Discovering soft wall after the panels arrive means either delay or covering a problem. Tap the whole wall before you spend anything.

Questions

Common questions

What is the standard size of a shower wall kit?

The most common alcove kits are sized for 60 by 32 inch and 60 by 30 inch openings, which match the footprint of a standard bathtub and the fiberglass units that replaced them. 48 by 36 is the next most common, and 36 to 42 inch widths cover tight retrofits. Panel heights are typically 72, 78, 80, 84, or 96 inches. Tub surround kits are usually built around a back wall up to 60 inches with side returns up to roughly 36 inches deep.

How do I measure for shower wall panels?

Measure each wall in three places — just above the pan, at about chest height, and near the top — and write down all three numbers rather than averaging them. Measure the height from the pan or tub deck to where you want the panel to stop, in at least two places per wall. Then check the corners for square with a framing square and the walls for plumb with a level. The spread between your three width measurements is the number that decides whether you can order exact-size panels or need oversized panels to trim on site.

What panel height should I choose?

72 inches is the traditional height and leaves a painted band above the panel on a standard 8-foot ceiling. 80 and 84 inches cover more wall and look more deliberate. 96 inches reaches the top of a standard 8-foot ceiling with no painted band at all, which is why it has become the popular choice — it removes the strip of wall that collects steam damage and needs repainting every few years. For 9-foot ceilings, 108-inch panels exist from a small number of manufacturers but are expensive and heavy.

What if my shower opening is not a standard size?

That describes most remodels. You have two options: order oversized panels and trim them on site, or order genuinely custom-sized panels. Oversized is usually the better answer because it is cheaper, ships faster, and absorbs the fact that your walls are almost certainly not perfectly plumb. Custom sizing makes sense for unusual layouts, very large showers, or when you want factory-finished edges everywhere with no site cutting.

How out of square can a shower be before it is a problem?

Up to about an eighth of an inch across a wall, exact-size panels will fit without anything visible. Between an eighth and half an inch is normal for a remodel and is handled by ordering oversized panels and scribing them to the wall. Beyond half an inch, the gap becomes visible enough that it is worth considering whether to fur out and re-plumb the wall before panelling, because a panel scribed to a badly leaning wall will look like a panel scribed to a badly leaning wall.

Can wall panels be cut to fit around a valve and showerhead?

Yes. Penetrations are cut on site with a hole saw sized slightly larger than the pipe or valve body, so the panel is not under stress around the opening and the escutcheon has room to cover the cut edge. Position matters more than neatness: measure from a fixed reference like the pan edge rather than from an adjacent wall that may not be plumb. The cut edge gets sealed, and the escutcheon does the visual work.

Does panel thickness affect whether my valve still fits?

It can, and this catches people out when going over existing tile. Every layer you add pushes the finished wall surface outward, and the valve stem projects a fixed distance. If the panel plus adhesive exceeds the remaining stem projection, the trim kit will not seat and you need a valve extension or the valve moved forward, which means opening the wall. Check this before ordering rather than after, because it can change which panel material you should buy.

Are shower walls ever actually square?

Rarely, especially in older houses and particularly after demolition. A quarter to half an inch of difference between the width at the bottom of a wall and the width at the top is entirely normal. This is exactly why remodeler's kits with oversized panels exist and why experienced installers order them by default. Ordering exact-size panels for an opening nobody has measured properly is the single most common way people end up with expensive scrap.

What size tub surround do I need?

Measure the back wall of the alcove above the tub and the depth of each return. Standard kits are built around a back wall up to 60 inches with returns to about 36 inches, matching the standard 60-inch alcove tub. For height, measure from the tub deck rather than the floor, since tub heights vary. 60 inches from the deck is the traditional minimum, 72 is the common modern choice, and 84 or full height removes the painted band above the surround.

Should I order exact-size or oversized panels?

Order oversized unless your three width measurements agree within about an eighth of an inch. The asymmetry is what makes this easy: a panel cut too large is five minutes with a fine-tooth blade, while a panel cut too small is scrap, a reorder, and two weeks of lead time. Professional installers order oversized as a default for remodel work, and only order exact-size for new construction where they measured the framing themselves.

How much wall area does a standard shower have?

A 60 by 32 inch alcove with 80-inch panels is about 69 square feet of wall across three walls. The same alcove with 96-inch panels is closer to 83. A tub surround at 60 inches high is roughly 45 square feet, and a compact 48 by 34 alcove is around 55. Knowing your square footage is useful for sanity-checking quotes, since panel pricing is normally quoted per square foot even when the final number is not presented that way.

What measurements should I send when asking for a quote?

Three width measurements per wall taken at the pan, at chest height, and near the top; the depth measured the same way; the height you want covered measured from the pan or tub deck; what is currently on the walls; the valve stem projection if you are going over existing tile; and your ZIP code. That set is enough to price the job accurately and to tell you whether you are in stock sizes. Sending an average instead of the three separate numbers is the most common omission.
